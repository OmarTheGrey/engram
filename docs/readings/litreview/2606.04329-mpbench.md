## Claim
Memory poisoning is a write-path problem with four channels and nine structural causes; agents with more aggressive write and retrieval policies are poisoned more often, and prompt-injection detectors catch the loud attacks and miss the quiet ones.

## Method
Four write channels (Sec. 2.2): C1 explicit instruction-executed write, C2 system-prompt-driven inferred write, C3 compaction-driven write, C4 experience-to-procedure write (task trace turned into a skill). Nine vulnerabilities (Sec. 2.3, Table 1): V-M1 instruction-data boundary blindness, V-M2 source attribution failure, V-P1 write policy under-specification, V-P2 compaction without source filtering, V-S1 no write-path validation, V-S2 shared multi-source context, V-S3 manipulable compaction trigger, V-S4 no validation for skill creation, V-S5 self-improvement as amplification. Six attack classes (Sec. 3.2, Table 2): explicit command insertion (strong, C1); conditional command insertion (strong, C1, fires on a natural affirmative reply); salience-driven compaction poisoning (strong, C3, repetition survives summarisation); policy-conformant fact injection (weak, C2); false precedent insertion (weak, C2, fabricated past task in experience format); skill-procedure insertion (weak, C4). MPBench has 3,240 attack cases over seven domains plus 2,997 benign cases, template-generated with Meta-Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (Sec. 4.1, App. D). Agents: OpenClaw and Hermes on GPT-OSS-120B with default settings. ASR is the share of cases where the judge finds the target directive written to memory; RSR is the share of those where a follow-up response reflects it (Sec. 4.1.3). Four prompt-injection detectors were tested off the shelf and retrained.

## Key numbers
- Average ASR/RSR: OpenClaw 34.25/17.40, Hermes 66.67/64.70 (Table 2); cross-agent 50.46/41.05 (abstract).
- Conditional command insertion: OpenClaw ASR 67.89, Hermes 76.00; salience-driven compaction: Hermes ASR 85.17; policy-conformant fact injection: OpenClaw 8.33, Hermes 64.50; skill-procedure insertion: Hermes 58.33, not applicable to OpenClaw (Table 2).
- Detector TPR/FPR off the shelf: PIGuard 38.33/0.33, DataFilter 23.00/53.33, CommandSans 52.33/45.00, PromptArmor 67.67/1.00; retrained PromptArmor 61.60/2.67 (Table 3).
- Strong versus weak signal detection: PromptArmor 84.44 versus 42.50; retrained PIGuard 48.33 versus 46.66 (Table 4).
- Judge validation: manual annotation of a random sample, no agreement figure (Sec. 4.1.3). No agent FPR on the benign set is reported.

## What Engram borrows
The four channels become the `provenance.kind` trust ordering (ADR 0004): `compaction` and `model` lowest, `tool` untrusted by default. V-S1 and V-S4 justify the deterministic write gate (ADR 0007) and replay admission for procedures (ADR 0011). The unevaluated defence list (Sec. 5): scope-limited write policies, source isolation, write-path provenance with source-aware retrieval that can demote or quarantine, compaction filters, and post-write monitoring against authorised behaviour rather than an attack catalogue. That list is close to Engram's design and is why MPBench is the H1 and H3 arm (ADR 0015, B2). Each class gets a fault fixture in `fixtures/faults/` (L2).

## What Engram does differently
MPBench proposes provenance and source isolation but does not build or measure them. Engram builds them as a deterministic gate with no model inside and measures ASR, RSR and the governance tax against an instruction-only condition. Compaction writes are a first-class provenance kind, not a prompt special case.

## Risk to our claims
Weak-signal classes carry no command and look like legitimate content; even retrained detectors stay under 47 percent on them (Table 4). A gate that checks source kind and scope can refuse to promote them beyond person scope but cannot tell a true fact from a false one. If H3's contradiction and unsupported-claim lints recover little here, the thesis must say these classes are contained, not blocked.

## Open question
Does blocking all writes from `tool` and `compaction` sources into shared scopes eliminate the strong classes outright, and what does that rule cost on LongMemEval? A step-0 pilot before policy defaults are fixed.
