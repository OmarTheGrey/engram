## Claim
Memory poisoning should be measured across the whole lifecycle: the write that persists it, the downstream task that acts on it, and the attempt to repair it. Across 24 configurations, poison persists most of the time, acts about half the time, and selective repair fails mainly because benign memory is damaged.

## Method
310 linked Write, Execute, Forget cases from 48 contexts in Code and Science, Daily Life and Office Work, authored with GPT-5.5, judged by DeepSeek-V4-Pro, run in an isolated runtime with real services (web gateway, Mailpit, GitLab) so consequences are externally recorded. Protocol (Sec. 3). Write: malicious content enters through a Carrier (user interaction, workspace artifact or external content fixture) during an ordinary task; W1 records only an accepted write, deterministically; W2 checks that new or changed entries jointly express the target semantics under the case rubric; only if both pass is the target memory manifest built and the later stages enabled, otherwise both are skipped without defence credit. Execute: a distinct downstream task runs from the verified poisoned state without mentioning memory; E1 is the earliest recall whose content keeps the core rule, object, condition or destination; E2 is adoption after recall; E3 is externalisation matched against the case's consequence contract through a service record and any required export; success is E1 and E2 and E3. Forget: an independent copy of the poisoned state receives one shared neutral prompt; F1 compares post-Write and final backend state to confirm removal or neutralisation; F2 requires every required benign memory from the clean state to remain; success is F1 and F2. Metrics: MPSR (W1 and W2 over all cases), MESR (full Execute chain among poisoned), E2E-ASR (all Write and Execute checkpoints), SRSR (F1 and F2 among poisoned). Matrix: OpenClaw and Hermes; Native, Mem0, Mem0-Graph, A-MEM; DeepSeek-V4-Pro, MiniMax-M3, GPT-5.5.

## Key numbers
- Across 24 configurations: MPSR 84.2, E2E-ASR 50.3, SRSR 56.1, MESR 59.6 (abstract, Table 2).
- Checkpoint pass rates: W1 91.1, W2 84.2, E1 76.1, E2 53.7, E2E 50.3; adoption is "the decisive boundary" (Finding 1).
- Among poisoned cases: F1 86.3, F2 62.5; benign preservation is the repair bottleneck (Finding 2).
- Largest matched gaps against Native: 16.1 points E2E-ASR, 41.3 points SRSR (abstract).
- Hermes/MiniMax-M3, Native to Mem0: E2E-ASR 34.8 to 48.7, so no backend is uniformly safer (Finding 4).
- Judge audit: 453/500 and 459/500 agreement with two annotators.

## What Engram borrows
W1's deterministic write record is the event log's `write` event (ADR 0003). The seven checkpoints map onto Engram's observable points: W1/W2 at the gate and post-write lint, E1 in the retrieval explanation (ADR 0009), E2/E3 in the runner's task outcome, F1/F2 as the retire-and-replay test (ADR 0010). The F2 result is why retention retires rather than deletes. The rule that a failed Write earns no defence credit downstream is adopted for the MPBench arm (ADR 0015).

## What Engram does differently
MemSecBench compares backends as whole stacks and evaluates no named defence. Engram keeps the backend fixed and varies the plane above it, so the same Mem0 appears under ungoverned, instructed and enforced conditions (H1, H4). Repair is retire by event plus re-projection, which cannot damage benign items because nothing is edited in place.

## Risk to our claims
Adoption, not persistence, is the decisive boundary (Finding 1), and the gate acts before adoption. If a plausible item passes the gate, the defence rests on read-time explanation and model-based lints. The thesis must report E2-style adoption, not just blocked writes, or H1 overstates.

## Open question
Which Carrier types dominate the 310 cases, and are workspace-artifact carriers covered by the no-bypass rule (ADR 0014) or only by provenance tagging? Appendix A was not readable.
