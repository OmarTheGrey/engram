## Claim
Agent memory is a write-manage-read loop in which one bad write propagates, and the field has moved from recall benchmarks to multi-session agentic tests. Governance (privacy, deletion, access scope, provenance) is one of five design objectives and one of four metric layers, but is the least evaluated.

## Method
A single-author survey of work from 2022 to early 2026, no experiments. Section 2 frames memory as the belief state of a POMDP and names five objectives: utility, efficiency, adaptivity, faithfulness, governance (2.3). Section 3 gives three axes: temporal scope (working, episodic, semantic, procedural), substrate (context-resident, vector, structured, executable, hybrid) and control policy (heuristic, prompted, learned). Section 4 covers five mechanism families; Section 5 reviews LoCoMo, MemBench, MemoryAgentBench and MemoryArena (Table 2) and proposes a four-layer metric stack (5.4); Section 7 is an engineering guide: write-path filtering (7.1), contradiction and staleness (7.3), latency (7.4), privacy and deletion (7.5), observability (7.7); Section 9 lists ten open challenges.

## Key numbers
- Table 1, Section 3.4: 16 systems and benchmarks listed.
- Section 2.4 and 5.2: on MemoryArena, swapping an active memory agent for a long-context baseline drops task completion from over 80 percent to about 45 percent; models near-perfect on LoCoMo fall to 40 to 60 percent.
- Section 7.4: retrieval typically adds 200 to 500 ms.
- Section 5.2, Table 1: LoCoMo up to 35 sessions, 300+ turns, 9k to 16k tokens per conversation.
- Cited from other papers in Table 1: Reflexion 91 percent pass@1 on HumanEval versus 80 for GPT-4; Voyager 3.3x unique items.

## What Engram borrows
- The four-layer metric stack (task effectiveness, memory quality, efficiency, governance with privacy leakage, deletion compliance and access-scope violations, Section 5.4) is the structure of the pre-registration's metric section (S0.12). Governance metrics are measured on GateMem and MPBench; this survey is the citation that they belong alongside task score.
- Section 7.1's metadata tags (timestamp, source, task, confidence) and 7.3's rule that user statements rank above agent inferences are exactly the `provenance.kind` trust ordering in ADR 0004.
- Section 7.3's temporal versioning and contradiction detection are the expired-validity and contradiction lints (ADR 0008). Section 9.1's "dual-buffer probation before promotion" is the candidate status in ADR 0011, stated independently.
- Section 7.7's memory diffs, replay and regression tests describe the policy simulator and the replay-from-events test (ADR 0003). Section 4.4's note that hierarchical virtual context fails silently is the argument for logging every decision with a reason.

## What Engram does differently
The survey lists governance as an objective and an engineering concern but reports no system that enforces it at the write and read boundary store-agnostically, and no benchmark of enforcement against instruction. Engram is that system and H1 is that benchmark. The survey's "learned control" axis is out of scope: the plane's gate is deterministic by design (ADR 0007).

## Risk to our claims
Section 5.5 states that long-context baselines remain competitive and that retrieval quality is the main bottleneck. If the memory layer itself is the weak link, governance over it adds a tax (H2) on a system that a long-context baseline already beats, and H3's recovered loss could be smaller than the gap to no-memory. ADR 0015 already makes long-context no-memory a condition; the survey is the reason that condition cannot be dropped.

## Open question
The survey separates "control policy" (who decides what to store) from governance (who may). Engram's gate owns the second; should it also constrain the first, for example by capping model-written writes per session, and would that count as enforcement or as a store feature?
