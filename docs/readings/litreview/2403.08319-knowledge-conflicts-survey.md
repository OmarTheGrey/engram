## Claim
Conflicts come in three kinds: context against parametric memory, context against context, and memory against itself. Models have no stable rule for which side wins, detect contradictions poorly, and favour whatever is coherent and relevant over what is credible or recent.

## Method
A literature survey with no new experiments (Ethics Statement). Context-memory conflict (Sec. 2) arises from temporal misalignment and misinformation pollution, including prompt injection. Inter-context conflict (Sec. 3) arises when retrieved passages disagree because of misinformation, including AI-generated text, or outdated documents. Intra-memory conflict (Sec. 4) arises from corpus bias, decoding randomness and failed knowledge edits. Detection methods for inter-context conflict (Sec. 3.3): specialised contradiction classifiers (PCNN, enriched XLM-RoBERTa, topology-augmented models), tool-augmented fact-checking (FacTool), and weak supervision over LLM-generated credibility signals. Resolution: a shared-encoder discriminator plus decoder (Hong et al.), query augmentation with per-answer confidence checked against retrieved passages (Weller et al.), and fact-duration prediction as a recency signal. The survey does not name source-credibility ranking, recency precedence or majority voting as methods; the closest items are Weller's answer-redundancy check and fact-duration prediction.

## Key numbers
- Models struggle to detect contradictions; Li et al. 2023a found average detection accuracy "subpar" and Wan et al. 2024 found models prioritise relevance over credibility cues (Sec. 3.2).
- Self-consistency of BERT-family models "barely ranging from 50% to 60%" (Elazar et al., Sec. 4.2).
- GPT-4 shows a 13 percent inconsistency rate on CommonsenseQA (Zhao et al., Sec. 4.2).
- Alpaca-30B consistent in only 50 percent of cases (Li et al. 2023d, Sec. 4.2).
- A 40 percent gap between knowledge-probe accuracy and generation accuracy (Li et al. 2023c, Sec. 4.2).
- All numbers are quoted from prior work; the survey ran nothing itself.

## What Engram borrows
Engram's contradiction lint is an inter-context conflict detector over stored items, so the survey fixes its shape (ADR 0008): detection is a model-based or NLI-style check between two items in the same scope, run after the write, emitted as a `LintFinding` with both item ids as evidence. Resolution is not the lint's job; it is policy, and the survey's catalogue gives the policy vocabulary: prefer the item whose provenance kind ranks higher (ADR 0004), prefer the later `valid_from` (ADR 0005), or flag for a human. Fact-duration prediction maps to `ttl` and `valid_to` as explicit fields rather than a model estimate. The finding that models favour relevance over credibility is the argument for putting the provenance rank in the retrieval explanation (ADR 0009) so the ordering is visible.

## What Engram does differently
The survey treats conflict as a property of the model's input at inference time. Engram treats it as a property of the store: a contradiction between two active items is a lint finding regardless of whether any query ever retrieves both. Engram also records which side policy chose and why as an event, which none of the surveyed methods do.

## Risk to our claims
The survey reports that contradiction detection accuracy is low even for strong models. H3 counts the contradiction lint as model-based, so if its precision on real memory is poor, the lint will produce findings that are themselves noise, and H3's recovery estimate must separate deterministic lints from this one (ADR 0008 already requires that split).

## Open question
Should the contradiction lint run pairwise over every active item in a scope, which is quadratic, or only between a new write and its top near-duplicates from the MinHash index (ADR 0018)? The second is cheap but misses conflicts between dissimilar phrasings, which the survey says are the hard ones.
