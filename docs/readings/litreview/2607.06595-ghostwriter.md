## Claim
A payload hidden in an ordinary inbound email is stored by almost every personal-agent memory system and later acts on about six in ten benign queries that retrieve it. A write-time admission policy scoring origin, trust, type and utility, plus a retrieval filter, cuts the attack rate sharply at small utility cost.

## Method
Threat model (Sec. 3.3): the adversary sends disguised messages, cannot touch the API, store or account, and aims at integrity corruption, leakage, exfiltration or scope escalation. Injection (Protocol 1): a payload embedded in a plausible message is turned into a memory by the agent's ingestion; success means it survives in stored form. Payload optimisation (Protocol 2) is black-box: cluster the Enron email corpus into topics and rewrite the payload until its similarity to the target topic passes a threshold. Activation (Protocol 3): a benign query retrieves top-k memories; if the poisoned one is present, the response deviates (the worked example redirects an email to a changed address). Agents: A-Mem, Mem0, ExpeL, Letta and MemoryOS, on GPT-5.4-mini, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Flash and Llama-3.1-8B (Sec. 6.1). AM-Sentry (Sec. 5.1) has three saving policies: S1, one LLM yes/no on future use; S2, an LLM scores origin (user 1.0, internal 0.8, client 0.5, untrusted 0.2, unknown 0.0), trust, type (instruction 1.0, workflow 0.7, preference 0.5, contact 0.1, fact 0.0) and utility, then fixed logic computes U = 0.6u + 0.4r and V = t(1 - o) and admits if U > 0.4 and V < 0.6 (Table I); S3 adds a second judge and source metadata. A retrieval screen R filters recalled memories for hidden directives.

## Key numbers
- Injection about 98 percent average; only Mem0 and ExpeL fall below 100 percent because they transform content before saving (Sec. 7.2).
- Retrieval about 94 percent on ChatGPT and DeepSeek, as low as 57 percent for Mem0 on Gemini; activation about 60 percent average (Sec. 7.2).
- Prompt injection: activation 0 to 16.7 percent overall; DataFilter 0 percent detection; PromptArmor 85 percent on directive payloads, 6 percent on descriptive (Sec. 7.3).
- AM-Sentry: S1 admits about 97 percent on ChatGPT; S3 about 15 percent attack success except Llama at 77; S3 plus R below 12 percent except Llama at 20 (Sec. 7.4, Figs. 8, 9).
- Utility: F1 0.01 to 0.04 below baseline with R or S3; Llama over-blocks from S1 (Sec. 7.5, Fig. 10).
- Per-agent cell values live in figures only.

## What Engram borrows
S2's origin ladder is a published precedent for the provenance trust ordering in ADR 0004, and its type ladder (instruction above workflow above preference above fact) is a reason to treat procedural-class writes from non-user sources as the highest-risk class (ADR 0007, ADR 0011). The descriptive-payload result, 6 percent detected, sits beside MPBench's weak-signal classes as evidence of H3's limits. The saving-policy and retrieval-screen split matches Engram's write gate and read-time explanation.

## What Engram does differently
AM-Sentry's scores come from an LLM judge at write time, so the gate contains a model. Engram's gate is deterministic: origin comes from the harness's provenance record, and a tool-sourced item is ineligible for shared scopes. Model-scored admission is where Engram puts lints, after the write, reported separately (H3).

## Risk to our claims
S2 still needs an LLM to fill its four fields, and the Llama results show a weak judge over-blocks. If the harness cannot tell "client" from "untrusted" in an inbox, the plane will admit too much or block all mail-derived memory. The thesis must state what provenance the pi integration can actually observe.

## Open question
Is a descriptive payload ("the colleague's address changed") a fact write the gate should allow at person scope with provenance `document`, so that the right defence is the source-changed or unsupported-claim lint rather than a write rule?
